# SEE-I Model

### Writing Concise, <mark style="background-color:red;">Impactful</mark> Short Answers

Have you ever struggled to clarify an idea, even when you thought you understood it?

You write your idea down on a piece of paper. Then you scratch your head and wonder, “What do I say?”

Sometimes it’s a lack of knowledge. But most of the time it’s because you haven’t thought critically about your topic or your reader yet. At this point, students often turn to Googling, keen to complete the answer, but later, will remember little, if anything. So how do we write better answers?

You can use a simple 4-part method to bring clarity to any idea so it’s easy to understand.

### It’s Called SEE

SEEstands for <mark style="background-color:orange;">State</mark>, <mark style="background-color:blue;">Elaborate</mark>, <mark style="background-color:yellow;">Example</mark>

It’s a step-by-step process for explaining an idea in a concise way. It reduces ambiguity and confusion, giving your readers a clear, easy-to-follow path through your ideas.

### How It Works

1.  **STATE your point.**

    Clearly state the concept or idea you're trying to communicate. Make sure it's brief and to the point.
2.  **ELABORATE on that Statement.**

    Explain it in your own words, as if you're talking to a friend who's unfamiliar with the topic.
3.  **Provide an EXAMPLE.**

    This is where you bring your statement to life. Use a real-world situation or create a hypothetical scenario that illustrates your point.

<mark style="background-color:yellow;">Plus, if you don't have an accurate 'technical' example, you can add on I - Illustrate</mark>

1.  **ILLUSTRATE with a Metaphor or Analogy**

    This simple comparison can clarify complex ideas and assure your readers truly grasp the concept.

Simple, right?

The reason SEE-I works so well is because it matches how our brains learn best. Stating the main idea first helps you focus on what you want to say. Elaborating helps you prove or back up the point you want to make. And giving real examples anchors your point and makes your idea easier to remember.

### Example

Suppose we want to explain the concept of "social proof." You start the process by first stating what you want to say. For example, “Today, we’re diving into social proof.”

Once you know what you want to talk about, then all you have to do is work your way down the list one section at a time. Here's the example in full:

#### Explain the Concept of "Social Proof"

**STATE**

Most people assume that if a crowd is doing something, then it must be ‘proven.’

**EXPLAIN**

{% hint style="info" %}
When you see a crowd doing something, you assume it must be the right thing to do. It's an instinct. Our brains are wired to follow the pack, to trust the masses, and to assume safety in numbers—often unconsciously. This is the power of social proof.
{% endhint %}

**EXAMPLE**

{% hint style="info" %}
For example, when a restaurant is full, you assume the food must be good. Or when you see a product on Amazon has 33,430 positive reviews, you assume it’s great. But, (counterexample) just because something is popular does not always mean it is good. McDonald’s is popular, but not necessarily good for you.
{% endhint %}

**ILLUSTRATE**

Imagine you are walking down a street with two ice cream shops. One has an extensive line out the door, while the other is empty.

Which one are you more likely to choose? The busy one, right? This is how social proof works.

Each section expands your thinking—and the reader’s comprehension. Here's the paragraph all together:

#### 🟩 **S — State the idea clearly**

> **Social proof is the tendency to believe that if many people are doing something, it must be correct or trustworthy.**

This is the core definition — brief and direct.

***

#### 🟦 **E — Elaborate in your own words**

> **This instinct is rooted in our psychology—we often rely on the behaviour of others as a shortcut for making decisions, especially in unfamiliar situations. We assume that the group knows something we don't.**

This expands on the definition and explains _why_ we follow others.

***

#### 🟨 **E — Provide an Example**

> **For instance, when you see a restaurant packed with customers, you’re likely to believe the food is excellent. Or when a product has thousands of glowing reviews on Amazon, it feels safer to buy. However, popularity doesn’t always equal quality—fast food chains like McDonald’s are hugely popular, but that doesn’t make them healthy.**

This shows how social proof appears in everyday life.

***

#### 🟧 **I — Illustrate with a metaphor or scenario**

> **Think of two ice cream shops side by side: one bustling with a queue out the door, the other completely empty. Most people will gravitate toward the busy one. That’s social proof in action.**

This visual and relatable image reinforces the concept in a memorable way.

#### <mark style="color:blue;">Good version</mark>

**Social proof** means that people often think something is good just because a lot of other people are doing it. Our brains are wired to follow the crowd. When we’re not sure what to do, we look at what others are doing and copy them. It makes us feel safer and more confident. For example, if you see a restaurant full of people, you probably think the food must be good. Or if a product online has thousands of good reviews, you’re more likely to trust it. But being popular doesn’t always mean it’s the best—lots of people eat fast food, but that doesn’t mean it’s healthy. Imagine walking past two ice cream shops. One has a long line of people, and the other is completely empty. Most people will choose the busy one. That’s social proof in action.

### Why is one better than the other?

#### **GOOD SEE-I Example**

* **State:** Gives a correct definition of the concept.
* **Elaborate:** Explains the idea in simple terms.
* **Example:** Provides a clear, everyday example.
* **Illustrate:** Uses a basic image or scenario to support the idea.

This is a solid response. It shows understanding and communicates the idea clearly. A good SEE-I might look like this:

> Social proof means we follow what others are doing. For example, if lots of people are eating at a restaurant, we think it must be good. This shows how people copy the crowd.

***

#### **EXCELLENT (OUTSTANDING) SEE-I Example**

* **State:** Precise and clear definition using appropriate vocabulary.
* **Elaborate:** Expands on the idea with a deeper explanation, including why it happens or how it works.
* **Example:** Gives a specific, relevant, and realistic example that makes the concept easier to understand.
* **Illustrate:** Ends with a strong metaphor, visual, or situation that makes the idea memorable and relatable.
* **Flow & Voice:** Uses varied sentence structure, avoids repetition, and maintains a confident, thoughtful tone.

Here’s what makes it better:

* **Clarity + Detail:** The explanation shows _why_ the concept works, not just _what_ it is.
* **Depth:** It considers both the instinct _and_ the limitation (e.g., popularity ≠ quality).
* **Engagement:** The analogy (ice cream shops) is vivid and easy to imagine.
* **Vocabulary:** Uses words like _trustworthy_, _psychology_, _instinct_, and _shortcut_—not just easy synonyms.

| Element          | Good Response           | Excellent Response                          |
| ---------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **Clarity**      | Defines simply          | Defines clearly with precision              |
| **Depth**        | Explains idea           | Explains _and_ explores reasons/impacts     |
| **Example**      | Real-world but basic    | Realistic, specific, and thought-provoking  |
| **Illustration** | Common or plain image   | Memorable, visual analogy or metaphor       |
| **Language**     | Simple, clear sentences | Varied structure and thoughtful word choice |
